# Paywall patterns: the evidence

Use this file to justify and size recommendations. Most of the evidence comes from mobile
subscription apps with large traffic (studies of a few thousand paywalls and interviews with
practitioners who design them for a living). A web product with a free core and a few signups a day
is a different situation. Say that when you cite a number: these are directions, not forecasts.

## 1. Timing and placement

- Users often decide before they see the paywall. Every touchpoint counts: after onboarding, when a
  premium feature is unlocked, in settings, as a win-back before leaving.
- Paywalls tied to a moment speak to the user more, because they are personal to what just happened.
- A well-built paywall at the wrong moment is still a bad paywall.
- A one-time offer that disappears when closed draws attention at the moment someone considers an
  upgrade. Use with care: if it becomes a pattern, users learn to wait for it.

**For a web product**: find the value moment (Phase 1 of SKILL.md) and put the offer right after it.

## 2. Paywall as a flow

- Practitioners report that multi-page paywalls almost always beat single-page ones.
- Selling the outcome first works. Example: a screen-time app showed users how many years of their
  life they could get back before asking for money; trial sign-ups went from about 7% to 17%.
- Information that unfolds step by step gives people time to decide instead of absorbing everything
  at once. When it works, the paywall feels like the natural next step, not a wall.

**For a web product**: step 1 is a live try or short video of the paid feature, step 2 the plans.

## 3. Risk reduction

- A reading app had users complaining that trials felt like a trick. It redesigned the screen as a
  step-by-step timeline (today, reminder, charge). Result: more trial sign-ups, fewer complaints, and
  more notification opt-ins, because people wanted the reminder.
- Making the trial prominent, adding a discount badge and making the offer easier to understand
  roughly tripled direct conversions in one case. The offer did not change, only its presentation.
- One app replaced the paywall with a single action ("redeem your free week"), modelled on Apple's
  redeem flow, and got about 25% more trial starts.
- A "no commitment, cancel anytime" subtitle under the button tends to lift results a little.
- A right chevron on the button shows up in most winning paywalls, though its effect alone has not
  been isolated. Cheap enough to include.
- An action-specific button label instead of "Continue" is mixed but worth trying.
- Exit-intent sheet: when someone cancels out of a yearly offer, offer the monthly plan.
- Instead of an aggressive last-minute discount, offer a longer trial. Same push, trust intact.
- **Card required**: one app started requiring card details for its free trial. Sign-ups dropped by
  more than half, but conversion to paid rose about five times, so paying customers more than doubled.
  The friction filtered out people who were never serious. A similar case: a trial offered only on
  the annual plan.

## 4. Framing and proof

- Social proof with real reviews and ratings builds credibility. Only real ones.
- Value framing: match the copy to what the user cares about, sell the future they want.
- Price anchoring: break the price into smaller units (per week), or compare it to things people
  already pay for.

## 5. Pricing and packaging

- One app tested 7, 14 and 30-day trials; 14 days on the annual plan won. The longer trial made the
  more expensive plan feel less risky.
- Defaulting to the yearly (or lifetime) product usually gives the highest lifetime value. Pair it
  with one shorter plan (monthly or weekly) and show only two, to keep the decision light. Further
  plans go behind a "view all plans" sheet.
- Keep the base paywall simple and learn its baseline numbers before optimizing every placement.

## 6. Formats

- Tables (free vs paid) are underused because they are harder to build in paywall tools, and they
  show well what the user misses without paying.
- A video of the product in action plus a short bullet list plus simple social proof communicates
  value faster than screenshots.
- Polish (animation, a consistent brand world) is increasingly what separates products competing for
  the same user.
- Design tests move the needle most: radically different layouts (video, bullet list, trial
  timeline, long-form) rather than small tweaks. An "ugly", text-heavy squeeze page has beaten
  polished designs, so there is no universal best paywall.

## 7. What to avoid

- Spin-the-wheel discounts work for short-term revenue, but they are spreading, users stop believing
  them, and they hurt long-term trust.
- Users increasingly close paywalls expecting an offer afterwards. Discount-on-close trains this.
- In early 2026 Apple began rejecting paywalls with free-trial toggles as confusing or misleading.

## 8. The long game

- Retention and lifetime value are the numbers that say whether the product is good. A paywall can
  only convert people who reached value; it can't replace a product worth paying for.
- Cancelling should not be harder than subscribing (a 5-screen signup vs a 17-screen cancel is the
  cautionary example). In Germany it is also a legal requirement for online contracts.
